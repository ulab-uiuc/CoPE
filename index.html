Abstract

Agentic reinforcement learning learns only from a terminal scalar reward, so most of a long rollout goes unused. Per-step value targets all derive from that one scalar, and next-observation targets are long, noisy, and reach only one step ahead. CoPE adds multi-step action targets: on successful trajectories, the policy backbone is also trained to forecast the actions that follow each history, so acting and forecasting co-evolve in one model. At inference it still acts one step at a time, with no overhead. Across ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, WebShop, τ2-bench, and AppWorld, CoPE outperforms GRPO and predictive-auxiliary baselines with up to 2.0× the sample efficiency, using no extra models, pretraining stages, or inference-time cost.

+5.0 success-rate points over GRPO on ALFWorld, +12.5 on WebShop, +8.0 on ScienceWorld
2.0× more sample-efficient than GRPO on ScienceWorld, 1.6× on AppWorld
0 extra models, pretraining stages, or inference-time cost — one backbone

Why action targets

Method

Acting helps forecasting via trajectory collection

The forecasting targets are not fixed labels. They are the hindsight action chunks of the successful rollouts that the current policy collects. For a fixed rollout policy π, the forecasting loss is minimized by the success-filtered distribution of the policy's own chunks: the chunks π proposes, reweighted by their probability of success, which the success filter enforces by rejection sampling. This keeps the target ahead of the policy that generated it: acting by the single-step marginal of that target succeeds at least as often as acting with π. Both factors depend on the current policy, so forecasting relies on acting to supply successful rollouts, and receives more of them as the policy improves.

Forecasting helps acting via implicit temporal ensembling

Read along the rows of the grid above, the forecasting loss asks each history ht to anticipate its chunk at:t+K. Read along the columns, every action at of a successful rollout also lies inside the chunks of the K preceding histories, so it is supervised from ht, ht−1, …, ht−K, not only from ht at the moment it is executed as in GRPO. These forecasts do not conflict with acting: all are conditionals of the same hindsight distribution, so the optimal forecast of at issued at ht−k equals the GRPO target marginalized over the intermediate observations. Because the forecaster shares its parameters with the acting policy, these earlier forecasts are implicitly ensembled into πθ through the shared representation.

The paper tests this directly: a separate LoRA forecaster is trained on the policy's own trajectories while the policy receives only the policy gradient, and its forecasts from several horizons are then used to rerank candidate actions. Explicit ensembling at test time improves GRPO and narrows its gap to CoPE, but adds nothing to CoPE, whose policy already contains that information in a single forward pass.

Results

Cost

Inference: no extra cost. The forecasting objective is used only in training. At inference the agent acts with πθ alone, one action per step and without the forecasting instruction, so a CoPE policy costs exactly what a GRPO-trained policy costs.

Training: +11% per step, −9% end to end. On ScienceWorld the forecasting pass adds 11% time per training step on average, while the shorter rollouts a better policy produces cut the measured end-to-end step time by 9% compared with GRPO. There is no separate model and no pretraining stage.
